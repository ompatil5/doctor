Type 2 Diabetes Remission

Can type 2 diabetes go into remission? What the evidence says

By Dr. Firdous Shaikh • 5 min read • Medically reviewed

Key points

  • Remission means blood sugar returns to a non-diabetic range without glucose-lowering medicines. It is not a cure.
  • It is most likely in people diagnosed recently, who lose a substantial amount of weight.
  • It needs regular follow-up, because diabetes can come back.

Remission versus reversal

I avoid the word “reversal” with patients because it suggests a permanent fix. The medical term is remission. An international consensus defines it as an HbA1c below 6.5% measured at least three months after stopping all glucose-lowering medicines [3]. If a person is in remission, the underlying tendency is still there. Weight regain, illness or ageing can bring diabetes back, and complication screening should continue.

What the research shows

The best-known trial is DiRECT, run in primary care in the UK. People with type 2 diabetes of less than six years' duration followed a structured, low-calorie diet programme with ongoing support. After one year, 46% were in remission, compared with 4% in the control group [4]. After two years, 36% remained in remission [5]. Remission was closely tied to how much weight people lost and kept off.

Two things to keep in mind. First, the study was in a largely European population. Indians and other South Asians tend to develop diabetes at a lower body weight and a younger age, so results may not transfer exactly. Second, people in the trial had strong support. Programmes that give less help usually see lower success.

Who has the best chance

  • Diabetes diagnosed within the last few years
  • Not on large doses of insulin for many years
  • Able to lose and keep off a meaningful amount of weight
  • Good support from family, and access to follow-up

Someone with diabetes of 15 years' duration and a failing pancreas has a lower chance. That is not a failure on their part. It is biology.

What it takes

The common thread is weight loss, mostly through a structured eating plan, regular activity, better sleep and attention to stress. In some people, medicines or bariatric surgery have a role too. These are decisions to make with your doctor, particularly if you take insulin or tablets that can cause low sugar, because the dose must come down as you improve.

What not to do

Do not stop your medicines on your own after reading an article, including this one. Do not follow a very low-calorie plan without medical supervision. And be wary of anyone who promises that diabetes can be cured in a few weeks.
