Pregnancy & Women's Health

Gestational diabetes: what to eat, and what to ask your doctor

By Dr. Firdous Shaikh • 5 min read • Medically reviewed

Key points

  • Gestational diabetes is high blood sugar first found in pregnancy. It is common and manageable.
  • Most women control it with food and activity. Some need insulin, and that is not a failure.
  • Women who had it have a higher risk of type 2 diabetes later, so follow-up after delivery is important.

First, take a breath

A gestational diabetes diagnosis is stressful, and many women feel guilty upon receiving the diagnosis. It is not caused by something you ate. Pregnancy hormones make the body resist insulin, and in some women the pancreas cannot keep up. Indian women are at higher risk, so it is a condition many of us will meet.

Why it matters

Uncontrolled high sugar in pregnancy raises the risk of a large baby, difficult delivery, low sugar in the newborn, and high blood pressure in the mother. Good control brings these risks down.

Eating

  • Spread food across three meals and two or three small snacks, so sugar does not spike or fall.
  • Choose whole grains, dals, vegetables, curd and nuts, and reduce juices, sweets and refined flour.
  • Keep portions of rice and roti consistent. A meal plan from a dietitian who understands Indian food works better than a generic list.
  • Add a 10 to 15 minute walk after meals. This alone often lowers post-meal sugar noticeably.
  • Do not skip meals or starve yourself. Ketones from fasting are not good for the baby either.

Monitoring

Your doctor will usually ask you to check fasting and post-meal glucose with a meter. Targets are set by your obstetric and diabetes team. Please write down the readings with the meal beside each one, because a log is far more useful than a number remembered from last week.

Medicines

If diet and walking are not enough, insulin is the most commonly used treatment, and some women may be offered tablets after discussion. Insulin does not cross the placenta in a harmful way. It is a safe way of protecting both of you.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • What are my glucose targets, and how often do I test?
  • When should I call you, and what number is an emergency?
  • Will I need extra scans?
  • When and how should I be tested after delivery?
  • Is breastfeeding recommended for me? (Yes, it usually is, and it helps.)

After delivery

Get a glucose test about six to twelve weeks after the baby arrives, and every one to three years after that. Women with gestational diabetes have a markedly higher lifetime chance of developing type 2 diabetes [7], which is why Dr. Shaikh maintains ongoing follow-up with patients after delivery.
