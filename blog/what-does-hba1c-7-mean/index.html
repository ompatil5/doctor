Diabetes Care & Glycaemia

What does an HbA1c of 7 really mean?

By Dr. Firdous Shaikh • 4 min read • Medically reviewed

Key points

  • HbA1c shows your average blood glucose over roughly the last two to three months.
  • 7% is a common target for many adults with diabetes, not a universal one.
  • Two people with the same HbA1c can have very different day-to-day glucose swings.

What the number is

When glucose circulates in your blood, some of it sticks to haemoglobin inside red blood cells. Red cells live about three months, so the percentage of haemoglobin carrying glucose tells us roughly how high your sugar has been over that time. That is all HbA1c is: a three-month average, written as a percentage.

Where 7% sits

An HbA1c of 7% corresponds, on average, to an estimated average glucose of about 154 mg/dL (8.6 mmol/L) [1]. For many non-pregnant adults with diabetes, 7% is the usual goal [2]. Dr. Shaikh notes “usual” because Dr. Shaikh rarely sets it as a universal rule.

A healthy 40-year-old with a new diagnosis may do well aiming lower, if it can be done safely. An 80-year-old with kidney disease and a history of low sugars is often better off a little higher, because a hypoglycaemic fall is more dangerous to them than a slightly higher average. Your target should be set around your age, your other illnesses, your medicines and what you can safely achieve.

Why the same number can mean different things

HbA1c is an average, and averages hide things. One person at 7% may sit steadily between 100 and 180 mg/dL. Another may swing between 60 and 300 and still land on 7%. The second person has more risk of both lows and spikes. This is why Dr. Shaikh often asks for a continuous or flash glucose monitor download, which shows the shape of your day and not only the average.

HbA1c can also mislead when you have anaemia, a recent blood transfusion, kidney disease, certain haemoglobin variants, or when you are pregnant. In those cases Dr. Shaikh relies more on direct glucose readings.

A note for South Asians

The widely used formula that converts HbA1c into an average glucose was built from studies with a largely Western population [1]. There is growing interest in whether it fits South Asian adults as well, and it is an inquiry Dr. Shaikh is evaluating in clinical research. For now, the practical advice is simple: do not judge yourself by the HbA1c alone. Look at your fasting and post-meal readings too.

What to do with your result

  • Know your last three HbA1c values and their dates, not just the latest.
  • Test every three months if your treatment has changed or you are above target, and at least twice a year if you are stable.
  • Ask your doctor what your personal target is and why.

References

  1. Nathan DM, Kuenen J, Borg R, et al. Translating the A1C assay into estimated average glucose values (ADAG Study). Diabetes Care. 2008;31(8):1473-1478.
  2. American Diabetes Association Professional Practice Committee. Glycemic Goals and Hypoglycemia: Standards of Care in Diabetes. Diabetes Care. Current annual edition (Section 6).
